"Copy what works" is the most repeated advice in short form video.

It is also nearly useless, because it skips the only questions that matter. Copy what, exactly? How do you know it worked because of the format, and not because of the creator, the timing, or luck? And how close can your copy get before the platform itself punishes it?

The operators who do this professionally have precise answers to all three. This is their system.

A big video proves nothing. A multiple does.

Raw views are the wrong score. A video can look huge and still be normal for the channel that posted it. A much smaller video can be the real signal.

⟦IMAGE: figure-outlier-multiple.png⟧

The smaller video is the lead. It beat its own channel by nine times.

This ratio, the video's views divided by its channel's typical views, is called the outlier multiple. It is the core metric of the whole strategy, because it separates the format's power from the creator's audience.

Working bands used by practitioners: below 1.5x is noise. Between 3x and 10x is a real outlier. Above 10x is a format breakout. Treat very high multiples on very small channels with suspicion, because a single lucky algorithmic push can manufacture one.

A format is only proven when strangers repeat it

⟦IMAGE: info-repeatability-test.png⟧

One outlier is a lead. It is not yet a format.

The second filter is repeatability: has this same structure produced outlier multiples on many different channels? A format that works for one creator may be riding that creator's face, voice, or audience trust. A format that works for twelve unrelated accounts has isolated the variable that travels: the structure itself.

This is the step most people skip, and it is why their cloning fails. They copy a hit that was never a format.

The two numbers that run this strategy

Here is the insight that separates the people who make this work from the people who try it once and quit: this strategy runs on two numbers, and almost nobody outside it knows either of them.

The first number is 20%. A typical clone returns a median of about 20% of the original video's views. Not 100%. Not half. Twenty percent.

⟦IMAGE: figure-twenty-percent-math.png⟧

One clone is not the bet. The set of clones is.

Read 20% as failure and you will quit in week one. Read it as portfolio math and the strategy makes sense: publish a set of clones, let them add up, and give one of them the chance to break past the original.

The second number is the budget: roughly 90% of output goes to cloning formats that are already proven, and only 10% to fresh experiments. Most creators run that ratio in reverse. They spend 90% of their energy inventing and 10% repeating what worked, then wonder why every week feels like starting from zero.

Hold on to the 20%. It changes how the entire strategy is judged, as you will see in the steps.

Step 1: Build the hunting list

Keep a tracked list of accounts in your niche and the niches adjacent to yours: competitors, accounts selling to the same customer with a different product, and your own back catalogue. Your own old videos count. A format you already proved once is the cheapest one to remix.

On TikTok, the Creative Center is the official hunting ground: top performing ads and trending videos, filterable by region, industry, and engagement. On the research side, tools like 1of10 and vidIQ's outlier views exist to compute exactly this multiple for you on YouTube and Shorts: views against the channel's baseline, instead of raw counts.

For every candidate video, record five things: the link, the channel's typical views, the video views, the multiple, and the date. A simple sheet is enough. The discipline is the tool.

Step 2: Break the video into a formula

Before you remake anything, take the original apart in this order:

The hook: the opening line, the first frame, and the curiosity gap, meaning what the viewer is promised but not yet shown in the first three seconds.

The beats: hook, tension, payoff, call to action, and whether the ending loops back into the beginning.

The packaging: shot style, framing, on screen text that works with the sound off.

The mechanics: length, where the payoff lands, the audio, and the exact call to action, including what it asks viewers to comment.

⟦IMAGE: figure-format-formula.png⟧

A format is a structure with variables. A video is just one expression of it.

Then run the transfer test. Write the format as a formula with variables: "Open on [surprising result]. Cut to [the mistake everyone makes]. Reveal [the mechanism] in three steps. End on [one word comment prompt]." If you cannot state it that way, you have not found a format. You have found a video, and copying a video is where the trouble starts.

Step 3: Clone it, changing one element at a time

Shoot everything yourself: your footage, your voice, your demonstration. Then change exactly one element per version: the hook, the call to action, the visual treatment, the language, or the product being demonstrated.

One change at a time is not caution. It is experimental design. Change everything and you learn nothing, because you cannot tell which element carried the result. Change nothing and you are no longer remixing, which brings us to the rules.

Step 4: Stay on the right side of the platforms

This is the part the "just copy it" advice never mentions, and it is enforced, not theoretical.

TikTok's own guidelines state that content is ineligible for the For You feed if it involves unoriginal or reproduced content without new or creative changes. A straight reupload is dead on arrival.

Instagram's rule, from its Creators team: accounts that post other people's content ten or more times in thirty days without creating it or enhancing it in a material way are removed from recommendations entirely. And when Instagram detects a near identical copy, it replaces the copy with the original in recommendations.

What counts as material enhancement: your own narration over it, a genuine remix, reaction, or parody structure. What does not count: a watermark, a speed change, or posting a screenshot with credit.

So the safe line is also the strategy's line: clone the format, never the file. Your footage, your voice, your product, at least one substantive change per version.

Step 5: Publish a portfolio, not a post

Publish five to ten clones of the same proven format. One clone is a bad bet. The set is the strategy.

Post them on accounts that stay inside one niche and keep a consistent look. Consistency is how both viewers and the algorithm learn what the account is. Practitioners generally suggest one to two posts a day for a new niche account, sustained over thirty days rather than in bursts. Treat that cadence as practitioner guidance, not a platform rule, because no platform publishes one.

Step 6: Judge the results by the strategy's math

Log every clone: which format, which element you changed, the views, and the multiple against your own channel's typical result.

Then judge correctly. A clone that returns 20% of a big original may still be several times your own average, which makes it a success worth repeating. The common failure is comparing each clone to the original, seeing a smaller number, and abandoning a system that was working exactly as designed.

Carry the winning elements forward. When a changed hook doubles the multiple, that hook becomes part of the formula for the next round. This is how a cloned format slowly becomes your format.

What this changes, one level deeper

Originality enforcement looks like a threat to this strategy. It is actually a filter that protects it. When platforms kill straight reuploads, the lazy version of cloning dies, and the value moves to the skill the platforms cannot penalise: extracting structure from other people's hits and rebuilding it in your own footage, voice, and product.

But notice what else happens. A format that anyone can clone gets cloned by everyone, and each wave of clones burns the format out faster. Detection speed and iteration speed become the real advantages, not the format itself. The format is a wasting asset.

Which is what the 90/10 split is really for. The 90% cloning buys reach today with proven material. The 10% experiments are the only part of the system that can produce tomorrow's format, the one everyone else will be cloning next month.

Copy to survive. Experiment to matter.
