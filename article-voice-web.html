"Copy what works." You have heard it a hundred times.

It is also almost useless, because it skips the only questions that matter. Copy what, exactly? How do you know it was the format that worked, and not the creator, the timing, or just luck? And how close can you copy before the platform itself comes after you?

People who do this for a living have clean answers to all three. This is their system.

A big video proves nothing. A multiple does.

Raw views will fool you. A video can look massive and still be a normal day for the channel that posted it. And a much smaller video can be the real signal.

The smaller video is the lead. It beat its own channel by nine times.

Take the video's views and divide them by what that channel usually gets. That number has a name: the outlier multiple. And it runs this whole strategy, because it tells you what the format did, separate from how big the creator already was.

Here is how to read it. Under 1.5x is just noise. Between 3x and 10x, you have got a real outlier. Above 10x, the format is breaking out. One warning: do not trust a crazy multiple on a tiny channel. One lucky push from the algorithm can fake the whole thing.

A format is only proven when strangers repeat it

One outlier is a lead. It is not a format yet.

So there is a second check: has this same structure already worked on a bunch of different channels? If it only worked for one creator, it might be their face, their voice, or the trust their audience has in them. When the same structure works on twelve unrelated accounts, the only thing left that can explain it is the structure itself.

This is the step most people skip, and it is exactly why their cloning flops. They copy a hit that was never a format.

The two numbers that run this strategy

Here is the part almost nobody outside this game knows: the whole strategy runs on two numbers.

The first number is 20%. A typical clone gets about 20% of the original video's views, at the median. Not 100%. Not half. Twenty percent.

One clone is not the bet. The set of clones is.

Read that 20% as failure and you will quit in week one. Read it as maths and the strategy clicks: post a set of clones, let them add up, and give one of them the chance to break past the original.

The second number is how you split your effort: roughly 90% of what you post should be clones of formats that are already proven, and only 10% should be fresh experiments. Most creators do the exact opposite. They spend 90% of their energy inventing new things and 10% repeating what worked, and then wonder why every week feels like starting from zero.

Hold on to that 20%. It changes how you judge everything, as you will see in the steps.

Step 1: Build the hunting list

Keep a list of accounts in your niche and the niches right next to yours: competitors, accounts selling to the same customer with a different product, and your own old videos. Your old videos count. A format you already proved once is the cheapest one to remix.

On TikTok, the Creative Center is the official hunting ground: top performing ads and trending videos, and you can filter by region, industry, and engagement. Tools like 1of10 and vidIQ's outlier views do the maths for you on YouTube and Shorts. They show you the multiple directly, views against what the channel usually gets, instead of just raw counts.

For every video you are considering, note five things: the link, what the channel usually gets, what this video got, the multiple, and the date. A simple sheet is enough. The discipline is the tool.

Step 2: Break the video into a formula

Before you remake anything, pull the original apart in this order:

The hook: the opening line, the first frame, and the curiosity gap, meaning the thing the viewer is promised but has not been shown yet in the first three seconds.

The beats: hook, tension, payoff, call to action, and whether the ending loops back into the beginning.

The packaging: the shot style, the framing, the text on screen that still works with the sound off.

The mechanics: the length, where the payoff lands, the audio, and the exact call to action, including what it asks people to comment.

A format is a structure with variables. A video is just one expression of it.

Then run the transfer test. Write the format as a formula with blanks in it: "Open on [surprising result]. Cut to [the mistake everyone makes]. Reveal [the mechanism] in three steps. End on [one word comment prompt]." If you cannot write it that way, you have not found a format. You have found a video, and copying a video is where the trouble starts.

Step 3: Clone it, changing one element at a time

Shoot everything yourself: your footage, your voice, your demo. Then change exactly one thing per version: the hook, the call to action, how it looks, the language, or the product being shown.

One change at a time is not about being careful. It is how you actually learn. Change everything at once and you will never know which thing carried the result. Change nothing and you are not remixing anymore, which brings us to the rules.

Step 4: Stay on the right side of the platforms

This is the part the "just copy it" crowd never mentions, and it is not theory. It is enforced.

TikTok says it plainly: content that is unoriginal or copied, without new or creative changes, does not get into the For You feed. A straight reupload is dead before it starts.

Instagram's rule, from its own Creators team: if an account posts other people's content ten or more times in thirty days without making it or genuinely improving it, Instagram removes that account from recommendations completely. And when Instagram spots a near identical copy, it swaps the copy out and shows the original instead.

What counts as a genuine improvement: your own voice over it, a real remix, a reaction, a parody structure. What does not count: a watermark, a speed change, or posting a screenshot and tagging the creator.

So the safe line is also the strategy's line: clone the format, never the file. Your footage, your voice, your product, and at least one real change in every version.

Step 5: Publish a portfolio, not a post

Post five to ten clones of the same proven format. One clone is a bad bet. The set is the strategy.

Put them on accounts that stay inside one niche and keep a consistent look. That consistency is how viewers, and the algorithm, figure out what the account is. People who run these accounts usually post one to two times a day on a new niche account, and they keep it going for about thirty days instead of posting in bursts. That is just what works in practice, by the way. It is not an official rule. No platform has published one.

Step 6: Judge the results by the strategy's math

Log every clone: which format, which one thing you changed, the views, and the multiple against what your own channel usually gets.

Then judge it the right way. A clone that gets 20% of a big original can still be several times your own average, and that makes it a win worth repeating. Where most people mess up: they compare every clone to the original, see a smaller number, and walk away from a system that was working exactly how it was supposed to.

Carry the winning pieces forward. When a new hook doubles the multiple, that hook becomes part of the formula for the next round. This is how a cloned format slowly becomes your format.

What this changes, one level deeper

All this enforcement around original content sounds like a threat to this strategy. It is actually a filter that protects it. When platforms kill straight reuploads, the lazy version of cloning dies with them, and the value moves to the one skill nobody can punish: pulling the structure out of other people's hits and rebuilding it in your own footage, your own voice, your own product.

But notice what else happens. A format anyone can clone gets cloned by everyone, and every wave of clones burns the format out faster. So what actually wins is not the format. It is how fast you spot the next one, and how fast you move on it. The format itself is a wasting asset.

Which is what the 90/10 split is really for. The 90% cloning buys you reach today, with stuff that is already proven. The 10% experiments are the only part that can create tomorrow's format, the one everyone else will be cloning next month.

Copy to survive. Experiment to matter.
