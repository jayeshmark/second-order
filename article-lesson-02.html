UGC for Products — Lesson 2

Lesson 1 covered what UGC is and whether it fits your product. This lesson shows you the destination: what a full UGC operation looks like when it is working.

A few paid posts are not a program

Here is how most products try UGC.

They pay for a handful of creator posts. The posts go out, nothing much happens, and they quit. Then they tell people UGC does not work.

Five posts is not a test of UGC. It is a coin flip. One video, or five, can land or miss for reasons that have nothing to do with your product: the creator, the day, the first two seconds.

A program is different. A program runs every week, on a system, with enough volume that luck stops being the main factor. That is what a creator army is.

The model: people post, not your ad account

Instead of your brand running ads, dozens of creators post native videos about your product. In their own voice. On their own pages. Every week.

Your brand stops being the channel. Your brand becomes the thing the channel keeps talking about.

This is not theory. The programs that run this way post numbers ads rarely touch. The team behind Halo, an app that grew on creator posts alone, claims zero to $300K a month in 45 days and 600 million views in two months, with no ad budget. Nomad Table, a food app, pulled about 44 million views in its best month. Those are their claimed numbers, and even if you cut them in half, no five post test gets anywhere near them. The gap is not talent. The gap is system and volume.

The three tests every video must pass

An army is not just more videos. Every video in the program has to pass three tests, or it does not ship.

Test one: built for views, not for selling. The video's first job is to be watched. If it opens like an ad, people skip it, and a video nobody watches converts nobody. Judge it like a viewer, not like a founder: would this hold you if you had never heard of the product?

Test two: it converts without a hard sell. Inside that watchable video, the product has to land. Not as a pitch. As the natural answer to the problem on screen. When this test passes, the comments ask where to get it. When it fails, the comments say nice video, and nobody buys.

Test three: the format repeats. Any creator in the army must be able to run the same format again, with their own spin. If only one gifted creator can pull it off, it is not a format, it is a performance, and performances do not scale. Repeatable is what turns one good video into fifty.

Most failed programs fail test one. They brief creators like advertisers, get ads, and wonder why the feed ignored them.

The three roles that run it

Behind the videos, three jobs keep the army alive. Miss one, and the program slowly dies.

The trend spotter finds formats worth copying. This person lives in the feed, watches what is breaking in your niche and outside it, and brings back formats the army can run. No spotter, and your creators are guessing. Your formats go stale while the feed moves on.

The creator coach makes the creators better. This person watches every video before or right after it posts, gives sharp feedback, and turns average creators into reliable ones. No coach, and every creator repeats the same mistakes forever. Coaching is where quality compounds.

The talent scout keeps new creators coming. Creators churn. They get busy, they get expensive, they fade. A program that stops recruiting starts shrinking the day it stops. The scout keeps the bench full so the army never depends on any one person.

Spotter feeds the formats. Coach raises the quality. Scout keeps the numbers up. That loop, running every week, is the whole machine.

At the start, one person wears all three hats

This sounds like a team. At the start, it is not.

At the start, one person does all three jobs, part time. Very often that person is you. You spot formats while you scroll. You coach in the replies and the DMs. You scout when you message new creators. That is enough to begin, and it is how the big programs began too.

Split the roles only when the volume forces it. When formats keep going stale, the spotter needs real hours. When creators stop improving, the coach does. When the bench keeps thinning, the scout does. Until then, three hats, one head, keep the loop running.

The deeper point

An ad account rents attention by the day. Stop paying, and you own nothing: no audience, no proof, no momentum.

An army is an asset you keep. A bench of creators who know your product. A library of formats that work. A feed presence that runs whether or not you opened an ads dashboard this week. Week after week, the feed fills with your product, posted by people, never by your ad account.

That is also why this lesson comes second. Every lesson after this one builds one part of this machine: recruiting, briefing, paying, measuring. You needed to see the whole thing first, so each part has a place to land.
